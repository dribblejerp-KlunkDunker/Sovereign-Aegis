## New Arena questions, grouped by the skill they test

### Spot relevance and distraction fallacies
`skill.fallacy.relevance` — 3 new item(s)

**q29** · difficulty 1120

> "The transport committee proposed a 15 km/h speed reduction outside primary schools. So they think cars are evil and want us all walking to work in the rain."

- **Answer:** Straw Man
- Distractors: Slippery Slope · False Dilemma · Hasty Generalisation
- Explanation: The actual proposal — a limited speed change near schools — is replaced by an absurd one that is easier to attack. This is not a slippery slope: no chain of consequences is claimed, the position itself has been swapped out.

**q30** · difficulty 1340

> "That statistical method was developed by a mathematician who later joined a eugenics society, so any result produced with it is tainted."

- **Answer:** Genetic Fallacy
- Distractors: Ad Hominem (Circumstantial) · Poisoning the Well · Guilt by Association
- Explanation: The objection is to the ORIGIN of the method, not to a person currently making an argument — that is what separates it from ad hominem. A technique's validity does not inherit its inventor's politics; that has to be shown mathematically.

**q31** · difficulty 1260

> Asked whether the audit found missing funds, the director replies: "What I will say is that this department has served this city for ninety years and our staff work incredibly hard."

- **Answer:** Red Herring
- Distractors: Straw Man · Appeal to Emotion · Begging the Question
- Explanation: The reply is true, warm, and about something else — the audit question is never engaged. It is not an appeal to emotion in the strict sense: no feeling is offered as EVIDENCE, the subject is simply changed.

### Spot presumption and structural fallacies
`skill.fallacy.structure` — 5 new item(s)

**q40** · difficulty 1450

> "If the server were compromised, outbound traffic would spike. Outbound traffic has spiked. So the server is compromised."

- **Answer:** Affirming the Consequent (Formal Fallacy)
- Distractors: Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc · Hasty Generalisation · Circular Reasoning
- Explanation: The argument fails by its FORM: many other causes produce a traffic spike. Post hoc is the tempting alternative, but nothing here rests on the order of events — even if the spike came first, the inference would still be invalid.

**q41** · difficulty 1470

> "If the account were a bot, it would post at machine-regular intervals. It does not post at regular intervals. So it is not a bot."

- **Answer:** Denying the Antecedent (Formal Fallacy)
- Distractors: Affirming the Consequent (Formal Fallacy) · Appeal to Ignorance · False Dilemma
- Explanation: Ruling out one signature does not rule out the category — modern automation deliberately jitters its timing. Distinguish it from affirming the consequent by which half is negated: here the IF-part is denied, there the THEN-part is affirmed.

**q42** · difficulty 1560

> "Every state-run outlet amplified the story. This outlet amplified the story. Therefore this outlet is state-run."

- **Answer:** Undistributed Middle (Formal Fallacy)
- Distractors: Affirming the Consequent (Formal Fallacy) · Guilt by Association · Hasty Generalisation
- Explanation: Sharing one property does not establish membership — plenty of independent outlets amplified it too. It is a syllogistic form error, close to affirming the consequent but stated over categories rather than conditionals.

**q43** · difficulty 1520

> "Either the footage is authentic or the timestamp was altered. The timestamp was altered. So the footage is not authentic."

- **Answer:** Affirming a Disjunct (Formal Fallacy)
- Distractors: False Dilemma (Bifurcation) · Denying the Antecedent (Formal Fallacy) · Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Explanation: An inclusive "or" permits both to be true: real footage can carry a doctored timestamp. False dilemma is the close cousin — that would be an objection to the two options being the ONLY ones, whereas the error here is in what follows once one is affirmed.

**q92** · difficulty 1460

> A question in a survey reads: "Do you support the failed and wasteful subsidy programme?"

- **Answer:** Complex Question (Loaded Question)
- Distractors: Loaded / Emotive Language · False Dilemma (Bifurcation) · Begging the Question (Petitio Principii)
- Explanation: Both answers concede that the programme failed and was wasteful — the presupposition is unanswerable within the question. Loaded language is present too, but the structural defect is that there is no response that does not accept the premise.

### Spot scope and quantity fallacies
`skill.fallacy.scope` — 2 new item(s)

**q32** · difficulty 1180

> "Both people I know who took that job quit within a year. It is clearly a terrible employer."

- **Answer:** Hasty Generalisation
- Distractors: Anecdotal Evidence · Fallacy of Composition · Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
- Explanation: A sample of two is generalised to an employer of unknown size. Anecdotal evidence is the close neighbour and would be the better answer if the anecdote were being used AGAINST statistics; here there are no statistics in play, only an over-broad leap.

**q33** · difficulty 1480

> An analyst reviews forty indicators, reports the three that moved together before the crash, and presents them as an early-warning signature.

- **Answer:** Texas Sharpshooter
- Distractors: Cherry Picking · Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc · Survivorship Bias
- Explanation: The pattern is drawn AFTER the data is seen — the target painted around the bullet holes. Cherry picking is the nearest miss: that would be selecting favourable data from a pre-existing claim, whereas here the claim itself is constructed from whichever indicators happened to align.

### Spot appeals to authority, emotion and crowd
`skill.fallacy.appeals` — 6 new item(s)

**q34** · difficulty 1210

> "A Nobel laureate in chemistry has said the new monetary policy will cause hyperinflation, so the case is settled."

- **Answer:** Appeal to False Authority
- Distractors: Appeal to Popularity · Ad Hominem · Appeal to Tradition (Argumentum ad Antiquitatem)
- Explanation: The credential is real but belongs to a different field. This is the harder version of the fallacy — the easy version cites someone with no expertise at all, while genuine authority misapplied is far more persuasive and far more common.

**q35** · difficulty 1150

> "This procedure has been standard practice in the guild for two hundred years. That is reason enough to keep it."

- **Answer:** Appeal to Tradition
- Distractors: Appeal to Authority · Appeal to Popularity · Begging the Question
- Explanation: Age is offered in place of evidence of effectiveness. Note that longevity is weak evidence rather than none — a practice that survived two centuries of use may work — but "it is old" is not the same claim as "it works", and only the second is being defended.

**q36** · difficulty 1290

> "The compound is plant-derived and completely natural, so it cannot be harmful in the doses we sell."

- **Answer:** Appeal to Nature
- Distractors: Appeal to Ignorance · False Dilemma · Equivocation
- Explanation: Natural origin is treated as evidence of safety. Hemlock, asbestos and botulinum toxin are all natural; the property that matters is dose-response, which the claim quietly skips.

**q37** · difficulty 1420

> "No study has ever demonstrated that the additive is unsafe at these levels. Therefore it is safe."

- **Answer:** Appeal to Ignorance
- Distractors: Appeal to False Authority · Shifting the Burden of Proof · Hasty Generalisation
- Explanation: Absence of evidence of harm is converted into evidence of safety. Shifting the burden of proof is the near neighbour and is often bundled with it, but here nobody is being asked to disprove anything — the gap in the literature is itself being treated as a finding.

**q38** · difficulty 1170

> "Four million subscribers cannot be wrong about this trading strategy."

- **Answer:** Argumentum ad Populum (Bandwagon)
- Distractors: Appeal to False Authority (Ad Verecundiam) · Astroturfing · Availability Heuristic
- Explanation: Headcount is offered as proof. Astroturfing is the distractor worth pausing on: that would require the crowd to be MANUFACTURED, and nothing here suggests the subscribers are inauthentic — only that their number is irrelevant to whether the strategy works.

**q39** · difficulty 1380

> "If this ordinance passes, ask yourself who will protect your children when the response times slip. Can you live with that?"

- **Answer:** Appeal to Fear
- Distractors: Slippery Slope · False Dilemma · Loaded / Emotive Language
- Explanation: A frightening consequence is asserted, not evidenced, and the question invites you to decide from dread. A slippery slope would need a CHAIN of steps to the bad outcome; this jumps straight to it, which is what makes fear the mechanism rather than the inference.

**q44** · difficulty 1600

> "All verified accounts have a badge. This account has a badge. So it is verified." — the arguer insists the reasoning is fine because the conclusion happens to be true.

- **Answer:** The form is invalid even though the conclusion is true
- Distractors: The argument is sound because the conclusion is true · The premises are false, which is the real problem · It is an informal fallacy of relevance
- Explanation: This is the point of the formal/informal distinction: validity is a property of STRUCTURE, not of outcome. A true conclusion reached by an invalid route gives you no reason to trust the route next time — which is exactly when it will mislead you.

**q45** · difficulty 1500

> A screening test flags 1 in 20 healthy people. A colleague reasons: "It flagged you, and it only flags 5% of healthy people, so there is a 95% chance you are ill."

- **Answer:** Base-Rate Neglect dressed as a formal inference
- Distractors: Affirming the Consequent (Formal Fallacy) · Conjunction Fallacy · Appeal to False Authority
- Explanation: The false-positive RATE has been silently swapped for the probability of illness given a positive result. It looks like a formal move but the defect is in the content: without the prevalence of the disease, the conclusion cannot be computed at all.

**q46** · difficulty 1400

> "His argument commits a fallacy, so his conclusion is false."

- **Answer:** Fallacy Fallacy (Argument from Fallacy)
- Distractors: Ad Hominem · Circular Reasoning · Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam)
- Explanation: A bad argument for a claim leaves the claim exactly where it was — unsupported, not refuted. This is the failure mode a fallacy taxonomy itself invites, which is why it belongs in one.

### Recognise confirmation bias in yourself and in sources
`skill.bias.confirmation` — 6 new item(s)

**q47** · difficulty 1230

> An investigator convinced the fire was arson spends the afternoon interviewing neighbours who reported seeing someone near the building, and never contacts the electrician who serviced the panel that week.

- **Answer:** Confirmation Bias
- Distractors: Availability Heuristic · Anchoring Effect · Fundamental Attribution Error
- Explanation: The search itself is shaped by the hypothesis — disconfirming evidence is not rejected, it is never gathered. That selective-search step is what separates it from the availability heuristic, where evidence comes to mind unbidden rather than being sought out.

**q48** · difficulty 1390

> Two readers are shown the same ambiguous polling table. Each reports that it supports the candidate they already preferred, and each can point to a column that does.

- **Answer:** Biased Assimilation
- Distractors: Confirmation Bias · Anchoring Effect · False Balance (Bothsidesism)
- Explanation: Both readers see all the data, so the bias is in INTERPRETATION rather than in what was collected — which predicts that shared evidence drives people apart rather than together. Plain confirmation bias is the broader family; this is the specific mechanism operating here.

**q49** · difficulty 1310

> A trader keeps a journal of every prediction that came true and remembers the losing calls as "the market being irrational that week".

- **Answer:** Selective Recall
- Distractors: Hindsight Bias · Sunk Cost Fallacy · Survivorship Bias
- Explanation: The record and the memory are curated after the fact. Hindsight bias is the neighbour — that would be misremembering how confident he WAS beforehand, whereas here the question is which events he retains at all.

**q50** · difficulty 1440

> Asked to discover the rule behind the sequence 2-4-6, a participant proposes 8-10-12, then 20-22-24, then 100-102-104, and concludes the rule is "add two".

- **Answer:** Positive Test Strategy
- Distractors: Hasty Generalisation (Secundum Quid) · Anchoring Effect · Conjunction Fallacy
- Explanation: Every probe is one the hypothesis predicts will succeed, so none could have falsified it. Hasty generalisation concerns too little evidence; the defect here is that no quantity of this KIND of evidence would help — the participant never tried 1-2-3.

**q52** · difficulty 1490

> A researcher pre-registers a hypothesis, collects the data, finds nothing, then reports a subgroup where the effect appears and describes it as the analysis she had planned.

- **Answer:** HARKing (Hypothesising After Results are Known)
- Distractors: Texas Sharpshooter · Selective Recall · Confirmation Bias
- Explanation: The move is presenting a post-hoc hypothesis as a pre-hoc one, which destroys the statistical guarantee the pre-registration existed to provide. Texas sharpshooter names the same shape informally; HARKing is what it is called inside a research protocol.

**q119** · difficulty 1420

> A hiring panel that liked a candidate's first answer spends the rest of the interview asking questions the candidate is likely to answer well.

- **Answer:** Confirmation Bias
- Distractors: Anchoring Effect · Fundamental Attribution Error / Ingroup Favoritism · Availability Heuristic
- Explanation: The interview stops being a test once the questions are chosen to be passed. Anchoring is a defensible second answer — the first answer did set an impression — but the diagnostic feature here is that the panel is now GATHERING evidence selectively rather than merely weighting it.

### Recognise availability and salience effects
`skill.bias.availability` — 6 new item(s)

**q53** · difficulty 1200

> After a week of coverage of a shark attack, beach visitors rate swimming as more dangerous than the drive to the coast.

- **Answer:** Availability Heuristic
- Distractors: Anchoring Effect · Confirmation Bias · Base-Rate Neglect (Prior Insensitivity)
- Explanation: Ease of recall stands in for frequency, and coverage drives recall. Base-rate neglect is arguably also present, but the ENGINE is vividness and repetition — which is precisely what makes this exploitable by whoever controls the volume.

**q54** · difficulty 1330

> Asked whether more English words begin with R or have R as their third letter, most people answer "begin with R". The opposite is true.

- **Answer:** Availability Heuristic
- Distractors: Anchoring Effect · Conjunction Fallacy (Linda Problem) · Confirmation Bias
- Explanation: Words are indexed in memory by first letter, so first-letter examples are retrievable and third-letter ones are not. The judgement tracks the accessibility of examples rather than their number — a clean case, since no emotion or media coverage is involved.

**q55** · difficulty 1410

> A security team fresh from reviewing a phishing breach puts most of next quarter into email filtering, while an unpatched external service goes unfunded.

- **Answer:** Availability Cascade
- Distractors: Confirmation Bias · Sunk Cost Fallacy (Escalation of Commitment) · Anchoring Effect
- Explanation: The most recent and most discussed threat crowds out the assessed one. It is not sunk cost — no prior investment is being defended; the distortion is that recency has been mistaken for risk.

**q56** · difficulty 1270

> A local paper runs six stories in a month about assaults in one park. Reported crime there does not change, but residents petition to close it.

- **Answer:** Availability Heuristic
- Distractors: Astroturfing · Hasty Generalisation · Loaded / Emotive Language
- Explanation: The frequency of COVERAGE changed, not the frequency of events, and the two are being read as one signal. Note that this requires no bad actor at all — ordinary editorial choices produce it.

**q57** · difficulty 1360

> A drug described as having "90% survival" is accepted far more readily than the same drug described as having "10% mortality".

- **Answer:** Framing Effect
- Distractors: Availability Heuristic · Anchoring Effect · Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Explanation: The numbers are identical, so nothing about the evidence changed — only which outcome the wording makes easy to picture. It sits beside availability rather than inside it: availability concerns what comes to mind, framing what the phrasing puts there.

**q58** · difficulty 1460

> An executive says air travel has become far more dangerous, citing three crashes she can name from the past year — none of which she could have named a year ago.

- **Answer:** Availability Heuristic
- Distractors: Hasty Generalisation (Secundum Quid) · Confirmation Bias · Survivorship Bias
- Explanation: That she can now name them is the whole of the evidence, and namability grew because coverage did. Hasty generalisation tempts, but the flaw is not sample SIZE — it is that the sample is drawn from memory, which is not a random sampler.

### Recognise anchoring and framing effects
`skill.bias.anchoring` — 6 new item(s)

**q59** · difficulty 1190

> A negotiator opens with a figure far above any plausible settlement. The final agreement lands well above what either side privately expected.

- **Answer:** Anchoring Effect
- Distractors: Framing Effect · Sunk Cost Fallacy · Appeal to Authority
- Explanation: The opening number does not need to be credible to work; it only needs to be first. Framing is the neighbour — that would require the same value described differently, whereas here a single irrelevant number drags the whole scale.

**q60** · difficulty 1300

> Asked "was the population above or below 20 million?" and then to estimate it, respondents guess far higher than a group asked "above or below 2 million?" first.

- **Answer:** Anchoring Effect
- Distractors: Availability Heuristic · Confirmation Bias · Conjunction Fallacy
- Explanation: The comparison figure carries no information about the answer, yet it sets the range people adjust within — and adjustment is reliably insufficient. This is the cleanest demonstration precisely because the anchor is transparently arbitrary.

**q61** · difficulty 1420

> A headline reads "Department seeks $4bn — critics say $3.2bn is enough". Later coverage debates the gap. The prior year's budget was $900m.

- **Answer:** Anchoring Effect
- Distractors: False Balance (Bothsidesism) · Loaded / Emotive Language · Availability Heuristic
- Explanation: Both published figures sit far above the historical baseline, so the "sceptical" number still anchors the debate high. False balance is a real distractor here, but the two positions are not being presented as equally supported — the distortion is in the numeric range they jointly establish.

**q62** · difficulty 1250

> A menu lists a $180 tasting option at the top. Sales of the $95 option rise sharply, although it did not change.

- **Answer:** Anchoring Effect
- Distractors: Framing Effect · Argumentum ad Populum (Bandwagon) · Appeal to Nature
- Explanation: The expensive item may sell rarely and still earn its place by re-scaling every other price. Nothing about the $95 dish changed — only the reference point it is judged against.

**q63** · difficulty 1470

> An analyst's first estimate of a breach at 40,000 records is later shown to be far too low, but every subsequent revision lands between 45,000 and 60,000. The eventual figure is 2.1 million.

- **Answer:** Insufficient Adjustment from an anchor
- Distractors: Confirmation Bias · Availability Heuristic · Hindsight Bias (Knew-It-All-Along Effect)
- Explanation: Revisions crawl away from the initial figure instead of being re-derived from scratch. Confirmation bias is close, but the analyst is not defending a conclusion — she is updating, just far too slowly and always from the same starting point.

**q64** · difficulty 1380

> A survey asks "how much would you pay to avoid this outcome, given others paid around $50?" before recording the respondent's own figure.

- **Answer:** Anchoring Effect
- Distractors: Argumentum ad Populum (Bandwagon) · Framing Effect · Astroturfing
- Explanation: The reference is doing two jobs — social proof and numeric anchor — but the measurable distortion is the numeric one, which appears even when respondents are told the figure was randomly generated. Bandwagon would require the crowd itself to be the reason offered.

### Recognise attribution errors and ingroup favouritism
`skill.bias.attribution` — 7 new item(s)

**q65** · difficulty 1220

> A driver cut off in traffic mutters "what a selfish idiot". When he does the same thing an hour later, it is because he is late for a hospital appointment.

- **Answer:** Fundamental Attribution Error / Ingroup Favoritism
- Distractors: Confirmation Bias · Hindsight Bias · Availability Heuristic
- Explanation: Others' behaviour is explained by character, one's own by circumstance. The asymmetry is the diagnostic feature — either explanation alone could be reasonable; holding both at once is not.

**q66** · difficulty 1340

> When our side's official misstates a figure it is "a briefing error under pressure". When theirs does, it is "a deliberate attempt to mislead the public".

- **Answer:** Ingroup Attribution Asymmetry
- Distractors: Fundamental Attribution Error / Ingroup Favoritism · Loaded / Emotive Language · Whataboutism (Tu Quoque)
- Explanation: The same act receives a situational reading for the in-group and a dispositional one for the out-group. The general attribution error is the parent category; naming the in-group/out-group split matters because it is what makes the bias politically load-bearing.

**q67** · difficulty 1450

> A manager reads a team's missed deadline as a motivation problem, without checking that the upstream dependency shipped three weeks late.

- **Answer:** Fundamental Attribution Error / Ingroup Favoritism
- Distractors: Confirmation Bias · Base-Rate Neglect · Hindsight Bias
- Explanation: Dispositional explanation is reached for first, and the situational one is never tested. Confirmation bias would require a prior hypothesis being defended; here the leap to character happens before any hypothesis exists.

**q68** · difficulty 1290

> A profile explains a founder's success entirely through her discipline and vision, mentioning in passing that the family funded the first two years.

- **Answer:** Dispositional Over-Attribution
- Distractors: Survivorship Bias · Availability Heuristic (Ease of Recall) · Appeal to Authority
- Explanation: Traits are credited with an outcome that had substantial situational support. Survivorship bias is the tempting alternative and would apply to a claim about founders in general; this is about the causal account of one case.

**q69** · difficulty 1510

> A commentator argues that because a hostile state benefits from a protest movement, the protesters must be acting on its instructions.

- **Answer:** Cui Bono Fallacy
- Distractors: Guilt by Association · Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc · Astroturfing
- Explanation: Benefit is treated as evidence of authorship. Astroturfing is the sharp distractor: that is a real phenomenon and would need evidence of coordination — account clustering, funding, synchronised messaging — none of which "someone benefits" supplies.

**q70** · difficulty 1400

> A reviewer marks a colleague's error as carelessness and their own identical error, found the next day, as a symptom of an unclear spec.

- **Answer:** Actor-Observer Asymmetry
- Distractors: Fundamental Attribution Error / Ingroup Favoritism · Selective Recall · Hindsight Bias
- Explanation: The precise form is the SELF versus other split, rather than in-group versus out-group. Both are attribution errors; distinguishing them matters because they have different remedies — one is fixed by perspective-taking, the other by rules applied before you know whose work it is.

**q71** · difficulty 1330

> After an outage, the report concludes "human error by the on-call engineer" and closes. The runbook had been wrong since March.

- **Answer:** Dispositional Over-Attribution
- Distractors: Hindsight Bias · Confirmation Bias · Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam)
- Explanation: Attributing the failure to a person ends the investigation exactly where the systemic cause begins. Hindsight bias is likely present too, but the load-bearing error is locating the cause in a character rather than in the conditions.

### Recognise probability and sunk-cost errors
`skill.bias.probability` — 6 new item(s)

**q45** · difficulty 1500

> A screening test flags 1 in 20 healthy people. A colleague reasons: "It flagged you, and it only flags 5% of healthy people, so there is a 95% chance you are ill."

- **Answer:** Base-Rate Neglect dressed as a formal inference
- Distractors: Affirming the Consequent (Formal Fallacy) · Conjunction Fallacy · Appeal to False Authority
- Explanation: The false-positive RATE has been silently swapped for the probability of illness given a positive result. It looks like a formal move but the defect is in the content: without the prevalence of the disease, the conclusion cannot be computed at all.

**q51** · difficulty 1350

> A team reviewing a failed launch reads the pre-launch risk memo and finds that it "obviously flagged the problem", though at the time nobody acted on it.

- **Answer:** Hindsight Bias
- Distractors: Confirmation Bias · Selective Recall · Fundamental Attribution Error / Ingroup Favoritism
- Explanation: Knowing the outcome makes the warning legible in a way it was not beforehand. This is why post-incident reviews asking "who missed this" produce worse findings than ones asking "what was visible at the time".

**q72** · difficulty 1430

> "She is quiet, reads constantly, and loves order. Is she more likely to be a librarian, or a librarian who plays in a band?"

- **Answer:** Conjunction Fallacy
- Distractors: Base-Rate Neglect · Representativeness Heuristic · Availability Heuristic
- Explanation: A conjunction can never be more probable than either of its parts, however well the extra detail fits the picture. Representativeness is the MECHANISM that produces the error; the conjunction violation is the error itself, which is what the question asks for.

**q73** · difficulty 1520

> A test is 99% accurate for a condition affecting 1 in 10,000 people. A positive result comes back. A colleague says "so it is 99% certain".

- **Answer:** Base-Rate Neglect
- Distractors: Conjunction Fallacy · Anchoring Effect · Appeal to False Authority
- Explanation: With that prevalence, the great majority of positives are false positives — roughly 1% of the healthy 9,999 outnumbers the single true case. The test's accuracy is real; the mistake is using it without the prior.

**q74** · difficulty 1280

> "Red has come up six times in a row, so black is due."

- **Answer:** Gambler's Fallacy
- Distractors: Base-Rate Neglect · Hindsight Bias · Availability Heuristic
- Explanation: Independent trials have no memory. Note the mirror-image error is equally common — concluding that red is "hot" and will continue — and both come from expecting short runs to look like long-run averages.

**q75** · difficulty 1470

> A programme is judged effective because the schools that adopted it improved. Schools that adopted it and closed before the follow-up were not counted.

- **Answer:** Survivorship Bias
- Distractors: Selective Recall · Base-Rate Neglect · Texas Sharpshooter
- Explanation: The sample is defined by having survived to be measured, so the failures are structurally invisible. Cherry picking would require someone choosing which data to show; here nobody chose — the selection is done by the measurement itself, which makes it much harder to notice.

### Identify astroturfing and inauthentic coordination
`skill.tactic.astroturfing` — 5 new item(s)

**q76** · difficulty 1350

> Four hundred accounts supporting a zoning proposal were created within the same eleven-day window last year, and share a posting-time distribution unlike the platform baseline.

- **Answer:** Astroturfing (Coordinated Account Creation)
- Distractors: Argumentum ad Populum (Bandwagon) · Brigading · Availability Heuristic

### Identify flooding, Gish gallop and epistemic exhaustion
`skill.tactic.flooding` — 5 new item(s)

**q79** · difficulty 1310

> In a ninety-second answer, a speaker makes nineteen separate factual assertions, four of which are false and none of which the opponent has time to address.

- **Answer:** Gish Gallop
- Distractors: Red Herring · Straw Man · Whataboutism (Tu Quoque)
- Explanation: The asymmetry between assertion cost and refutation cost is the weapon; the individual claims barely matter. A red herring diverts to ONE other topic, which is a different and much cheaper attack to answer.

**q80** · difficulty 1480

> After a leak, a network publishes six mutually incompatible explanations within a day, none pushed harder than the others.

- **Answer:** Firehose of Falsehood
- Distractors: Gish Gallop · False Balance (Bothsidesism) · Appeal to Ignorance
- Explanation: The goal is not to be believed but to make belief feel arbitrary — mutual incompatibility is a feature, since a consistent lie could be refuted. A Gish gallop tries to WIN an exchange; this tries to exhaust the possibility of one.

**q81** · difficulty 1390

> A records request is answered with 40,000 unsorted, unsearchable scanned pages within the statutory deadline.

- **Answer:** Epistemic Flooding by Compliance
- Distractors: Gish Gallop · Red Herring · Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam)
- Explanation: The request is technically satisfied while the answer is made unreachable. It shares its mechanism with the Gish gallop — cost asymmetry — but happens in a procedural rather than rhetorical setting, which is why it is rarely recognised as the same move.

**q82** · difficulty 1420

- **Answer:** Brigading
- Distractors: Astroturfing · Gish Gallop · Coordinated Inauthentic Behaviour
- Explanation: Volume is aimed at a process rather than at an audience — the target is the moderation system's capacity. It overlaps with coordinated inauthentic behaviour, but brigading can be entirely authentic people acting together, which is what makes it hard to police.

**q108** · difficulty 1310

- **Answer:** T0048: Flood the Information Environment / Epistemic Pollution
- Distractors: Narrative Laundering · Gish Gallop · Brigading

### Identify false balance and manufactured doubt
`skill.tactic.false-balance` — 7 new item(s)

**q83** · difficulty 1360

> A segment on a well-replicated finding gives equal airtime to the one researcher who disputes it and to the consensus position, without saying how many hold each.

- **Answer:** False Balance (Bothsidesism)
- Distractors: Appeal to False Authority · Argumentum ad Populum (Bandwagon) · Manufactured Doubt
- Explanation: Equal time misrepresents the state of the evidence as an even split. Manufactured doubt is the strategy this often serves, but that names a deliberate campaign; false balance is the editorial artefact, which frequently occurs without one.

**q84** · difficulty 1550

- **Answer:** Manufactured Doubt
- Distractors: False Balance (Bothsidesism) · Appeal to Ignorance · Firehose of Falsehood
- Explanation: The objective is uncertainty itself, because inaction follows from it — no positive counter-claim needs to survive scrutiny. False balance is the media surface this strategy exploits, not the strategy.

**q85** · difficulty 1280

- **Answer:** False Balance (Bothsidesism)
- Distractors: Appeal to False Authority (Ad Verecundiam) · Red Herring · Ad Hominem
- Explanation: The pairing implies comparable standing on a question where it does not exist. Appeal to false authority is the near miss and would apply if the COLUMNIST'S credentials were being cited as support; here the fallacy is committed by the framing, not by either speaker.

**q86** · difficulty 1470

- Distractors: Cherry Picking · Hasty Generalisation · Availability Heuristic (Ease of Recall)
- Explanation: A 2-to-2 presentation of a lopsided literature manufactures a division. Cherry picking is genuinely also present, which is worth noticing: false balance is usually implemented THROUGH selective citation rather than being an alternative to it.

**q87** · difficulty 1400

- **Answer:** Neutrality as an evasion of editorial judgement
- Distractors: False Balance (Bothsidesism) · Appeal to Ignorance · Argumentum ad Populum (Bandwagon)
- Explanation: Deciding what counts as a "position" worth airing is already an editorial judgement; declining to weigh evidence does not avoid influence, it just hides where the influence was applied. **q88** · difficulty 1520

### Identify loaded language and emotive framing
`skill.tactic.emotive-framing` — 6 new item(s)

**q39** · difficulty 1380

> "If this ordinance passes, ask yourself who will protect your children when the response times slip. Can you live with that?"

- **Answer:** Appeal to Fear
- Distractors: Slippery Slope · False Dilemma · Loaded / Emotive Language
- Explanation: A frightening consequence is asserted, not evidenced, and the question invites you to decide from dread. A slippery slope would need a CHAIN of steps to the bad outcome; this jumps straight to it, which is what makes fear the mechanism rather than the inference.

**q57** · difficulty 1360

> A drug described as having "90% survival" is accepted far more readily than the same drug described as having "10% mortality".

- **Answer:** Framing Effect
- Distractors: Availability Heuristic · Anchoring Effect · Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Explanation: The numbers are identical, so nothing about the evidence changed — only which outcome the wording makes easy to picture. It sits beside availability rather than inside it: availability concerns what comes to mind, framing what the phrasing puts there.

**q90** · difficulty 1240

- **Answer:** Loaded / Emotive Language
- Distractors: Framing Effect · Equivocation (Semantic Shift) · Straw Man
- Explanation: The verb imports a verdict before any argument is made. It differs from equivocation in that no term shifts meaning WITHIN an argument — the word choice smuggles the conclusion in at the start.

**q91** · difficulty 1370

- **Answer:** Loaded / Emotive Language
- Distractors: Appeal to Fear · Hasty Generalisation · Availability Heuristic (Ease of Recall)
- Explanation: Metaphor does the work of evidence: "flood" implies volume, threat and lack of agency simultaneously. Appeal to fear is the neighbour — that names an explicit frightening consequence, whereas here the fear is carried by vocabulary alone.

**q92** · difficulty 1460

- **Answer:** Complex Question (Loaded Question)
- Distractors: Loaded / Emotive Language · False Dilemma (Bifurcation) · Begging the Question (Petitio Principii)
- Explanation: Both answers concede that the programme failed and was wasteful — the presupposition is unanswerable within the question. Loaded language is present too, but the structural defect is that there is no response that does not accept the premise.

**q93** · difficulty 1330

- **Answer:** Loaded / Emotive Language
- Distractors: Ingroup Attribution Asymmetry · Astroturfing · Whataboutism (Tu Quoque)
- Explanation: The same behaviour receives opposite labels. There is an attribution asymmetry underneath, which makes that a defensible second choice — but what is being asked about is the vocabulary doing the work, and the question is which lever is being pulled on the reader.

### Detect equivocation and semantic shift
`skill.tactic.equivocation` — 6 new item(s)

**q94** · difficulty 1350

> "The report says the risk is not significant. So there is no meaningful risk to residents."

- **Answer:** Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Distractors: Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam) · Hasty Generalisation · Straw Man
- Explanation: "Significant" moves from its statistical sense to its everyday one between the two sentences. A statistically non-significant result can describe a large and worrying effect measured imprecisely — the terms are not interchangeable, which is exactly what the argument relies on.

**q95** · difficulty 1490

> "Every socialist regime has collapsed." Shown a counterexample: "That one was not truly socialist."

- **Answer:** No True Scotsman
- Distractors: Equivocation (Semantic Shift) · Circular Reasoning · Begging the Question
- Explanation: The category is redefined mid-argument so the claim cannot be tested. It is a specific form of semantic shift, and worth distinguishing: ordinary equivocation exploits an existing ambiguity, while this one MANUFACTURES the ambiguity in response to evidence.

**q96** · difficulty 1420

> "The data is anonymised — no names are stored. So the dataset cannot identify anyone."

- **Answer:** Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Distractors: Appeal to Ignorance · Hasty Generalisation · Affirming the Consequent (Formal Fallacy)
- Explanation: "Anonymised" is used first in the narrow sense of name removal and then in the strong sense of non-identifiability, which are famously not the same — postcode, birth date and sex re-identify most people. The word is doing two different jobs in two consecutive clauses.

**q97** · difficulty 1300

> "Of course the theory is only a theory — so it is hardly established fact."

- **Answer:** Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Distractors: Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam) · Straw Man · False Dilemma (Bifurcation)
- Explanation: The technical sense of "theory" — an explanatory framework supported by evidence — is swapped for the colloquial "guess". Note that the sentence contains no false statement, which is why equivocation is so much harder to answer than a lie.

**q98** · difficulty 1540

> A spokesperson: "We have never had a data breach." Later: incidents were logged as "security events" under an internal policy that reserves "breach" for confirmed exfiltration.

- **Answer:** Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Distractors: Appeal to Ignorance (Argumentum ad Ignorantiam) · No True Scotsman · Loaded / Emotive Language
- Explanation: A private definition is used to make a public claim that a listener will read under the ordinary definition. It is close to No True Scotsman, but nothing is being redefined in RESPONSE to a counterexample — the narrow definition was established in advance, which makes it more deliberate rather than less.

**q99** · difficulty 1380

> "Free speech means I can say what I like. So a platform removing my post is a free speech violation."

- **Answer:** Equivocation (Semantic Shift)
- Distractors: Straw Man · Appeal to Tradition (Argumentum ad Antiquitatem) · Slippery Slope
- Explanation: The phrase shifts from a moral principle to a specific legal constraint on government action, and the conclusion only follows under the second reading while the premise is only plausible under the first.

## New SIFT scenarios

**sift-stop-006** · stop lab · intermediate · Science

> The Royal Astronomical Society has published preliminary observations suggesting the comet's orbital period is about 340 years, revised from an earlier estimate of 290. The paper notes the estimate may shift again as more observations come in.

- **Correct:** continue
- Explanation: Nothing here is trying to move you. A named institution, a hedged claim, a stated revision, and an explicit acknowledgement that the number may change again — that is what ordinary science communication looks like. The stop reflex is for noticing an emotional pull; there is no pull here to notice. Treating this as suspicious is the mirror-image error to credulity, and it is just as costly: an operator who distrusts everything has not become discerning, only unreachable.

**sift-stop-007** · stop lab · advanced · Government

> The city transit authority announced that the Line 4 closure has been extended by three weeks. The notice cites a contractor delay, links to the revised schedule, and gives a phone number for affected riders.

- **Correct:** continue
- Explanation: Mundane, verifiable, and self-undermining in the way real institutional communication tends to be — nobody invents an announcement that makes them look incompetent. It names a cause, links a document and offers a channel to complain. There is no emotional lever, no out-group, and nothing being asked of you beyond information. Engaging with and sharing this is the correct behaviour.

**sift-stop-008** · stop lab · advanced · Health

> A meta-analysis in a peer-reviewed journal reports that a widely used sleep supplement performs no better than placebo across 14 trials. The authors disclose funding from a public research council and note that two included trials were small enough to skew the pooled estimate.

- **Correct:** continue
- Explanation: This is a negative result, disclosed funding, and a stated limitation that weakens the authors' own headline. Every one of those cuts against manipulation. It is worth noticing that the conclusion may be unwelcome — an operator who stops here because they take the supplement is stopping for motivated reasons, not epistemic ones, which is the harder version of this skill: the stop reflex must not be selectively applied to claims you dislike.

**sift-investigate-006** · investigate lab · intermediate · Economics

> An analysis piece states that regional unemployment rose for the fourth consecutive quarter.

- **Correct:** half_truth
- Explanation: The numbers check out — this is not fabrication, and calling it so would be an over-read. What lateral reading exposes is an undisclosed interest shaping which true facts get published and what conclusion is drawn from them. Selection is an editorial act, and a source can mislead without ever stating a falsehood.

**sift-investigate-007** · investigate lab · advanced · Science

> A paper reports that a common agricultural fungicide has no measurable effect on pollinator colony survival.

- **Correct:** reliable
- Explanation: Industry funding is a reason to look harder, not a reason to dismiss — and here the looking is what settles it: pre-registration, published data, and independent replication are exactly the checks that make funding less able to determine the result. Reflexively rejecting anything an interested party paid for would discard most applied science, which is a failure of investigation rather than a success of it.

**sift-investigate-008** · investigate lab · advanced · Media

> A breaking-news account with 400,000 followers reports an explosion at a named chemical plant.

- **Correct:** domain_spoof
- Explanation: The account borrows the credibility of a real outlet that no longer exists to defend its own name — the strongest available identity to steal. Distinguish this from fabricated attribution, where a quote is put in a real person's mouth: here the entire OUTLET is the forgery, and the account-age against follower-count mismatch is what makes it visible in seconds.

**sift-coverage-006** · coverage lab · intermediate · Government

> The city has "quietly cut" the winter shelter budget by 60%.

- **Correct:** opt-b
- Explanation: The primary document is not automatically the best coverage. It confirms the 60% but cannot tell you what the claim turns on — whether the money was cut or moved. The Herald piece is the better answer because it does the work the question requires: sourcing the number AND supplying the context that decides what it means. Going to the primary source is a step, not a finish line.

**sift-coverage-007** · coverage lab · advanced · Health

> A new blood test "detects fifty cancers before symptoms appear".

- **Correct:** opt-b
- Explanation: Here the primary source IS the right destination, because the claim turns on numbers only the trial reports — positive predictive value in a screening population is the figure that decides whether "detects fifty cancers" means anything useful. Contrast this with the shelter-budget scenario, where the primary document could not answer the question: which source is best depends on what you need from it, not on a ranking of source types.

**sift-coverage-008** · coverage lab · intermediate · Technology

> A messaging app "has been broken" and messages can now be read by anyone.

- **Correct:** opt-b
- Explanation: The advisory is upstream of both other pieces and is the only one that defines the scope — metadata under downgrade conditions is a serious but far narrower claim than "encryption broken". The cryptographer's analysis is genuinely good coverage and would be the right answer if the advisory were unreadable; the reason it loses here is only that it derives from the source you can reach directly.

**sift-trace-006** · trace lab · intermediate · Quotes

> A widely shared graphic attributes to a sitting central bank governor the line: "We will let inflation run hot and let savers pay for it."

- **Correct:** SATIRE STRIPPED OF ITS FRAME — Invented quote, real speaker
- Explanation: The origin is not a lie but a joke that lost its context on the way. This is one of the most common paths a fabricated quote takes, and it means the person who first wrote it may have deceived nobody. Tracing to origin is what distinguishes it from deliberate fabrication — and the real quotation it distorts is close enough to make the fake plausible.

**sift-trace-007** · trace lab · advanced · Statistics

> A chart circulating as "official figures" shows violent crime doubling in five years.

- **Correct:** ACCURATE NUMBERS, BROKEN COMPARISON — Truncated axis and a definition change
- Explanation: Every figure on this chart is real, which is what makes it effective — there is nothing to fact-check. The distortion is in the axis and in comparing across a definitional break the publisher explicitly warned about. Tracing to origin here does not expose a fake; it exposes that the honest source already documented why the comparison does not hold.

**sift-trace-008** · trace lab · advanced · Video

> Footage circulates showing a politician stumbling badly at a public event, captioned as evidence of ill health.

- **Correct:** AUTHENTIC FOOTAGE, DECEPTIVE EDIT — Nothing altered except where it stops
- Explanation: Forensics would find nothing here, because there is nothing to find: the pixels are real and unmodified. The deception is entirely in the cut, and the only tool that exposes it is finding the fuller recording. This is why provenance and origin-tracing matter even when a file passes every technical check — and why "no manipulation detected" is not a verdict on a claim.
